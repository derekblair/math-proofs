# Nonnegative bounded-degree projections of powered permanents

Let \(M_\ell(n,q)\) be the least order of an exact permanent representation of \(\operatorname{per}_n(X^{\circ q})\) by real polynomials with nonnegative coefficients and total degree at most \(\ell\) in each entry. Let \(n\ge3\), \(1\le\ell<q\), and \(L=\lceil q/\ell\rceil\). Then
\[
\max\!\left\{\left\lceil qn/\ell\right\rceil,\
1+\left\lceil(q-\ell)(n^2-n)/\ell\right\rceil,\
1+(L-1)\left\lfloor(n+1)^2/4\right\rfloor\right\}
\;\le M_\ell(n,q)\le\; n+(L-1)n^2.
\]
The first bound is degree. The second is a weighted modular forest argument. The third follows from the exact bounded-degree Fermat formula and Hall slice below. For the upper construction, split \(q\) into \(L\) positive degrees \(a_1,\ldots,a_L\le\ell\). Replace each input edge by a path of length \(2L-1\), with \(x_e^{a_1},\ldots,x_e^{a_L}\) on odd positions and constants one on even positions. Use distinct internal vertices for all paths. A perfect matching either uses all odd positions (matching both input endpoints) or all even positions (matching neither); these are the only ways to cover the internal vertices. There are \(L-1\) added vertices on each side per input edge.

For \(q\) divisible by \(\ell\) the leading coefficient is \(q/\ell-1\), with an additive gap at most \(qn/\ell-1\) and a relative \(O(1/n)\) gap uniformly. In particular there is a degree threshold: even degree-\((q-1)\) nonnegative polynomial entries need order quadratic in \(n\) for fixed \(q\ge2\). Degree \(q\) gives order exactly \(n\), by taking the input entry powers directly and using the degree lower bound.


For cubic entrywise powers and quadratic representing entries, the sharper bounds are **n²−n ≤ M₂(n,3) ≤ n²+n** for every n ≥ 2. They give the sharp leading coefficient one.

## Sharp leading coefficient for quadratic entries

Expand representing polynomials into parallel labeled matching edges, retain positive coefficients and used edges. Select a reference term and relabel original target columns so its output matching is diagonal. Its absent variables are (i,j), i≠j, M=n²−n. Contract the representing reference matching; each remaining arc belongs to a directed switch cycle, so no intercomponent arcs between SCCs after removing unused edges. On a directed simple switch cycle, each absent coordinate's total added exponent is 0 or 3 (reference supplies none and each switched matching produces a target monomial). Thus all closed walks sum to zero modulo 3. In each SCC there is a vector-valued potential in F₃ᴹ, using path sums and a common return to check well-definedness; for each arc the head-minus-tail potential is its absent-coordinate exponent vector mod 3. Loops imply zero differences. Choose an undirected spanning tree per SCC and remember an arc for each edge. Let A have these exponent vectors as columns; m−C columns for C SCCs. Tree telescoping shows every arc exponent vector mod 3 is in the column span of A, even if traversing some tree edges backwards.

For each c in the left kernel of A over F₃, lift its entries to −1,0,1. The integer dot product with an arc's absent exponent vector has absolute value ≤2 by the total degree assumption. It also vanishes mod 3 by the span argument, hence it vanishes as an integer. Reference terms have all absent exponents zero. Thus the integer dot product is zero for every labeled perfect matching. Every target permutation occurs as a labeled matching by positivity of its coefficient, so 3 Σ_i c_(i,σ(i))=0 as an integer on each σ; set c_(i,i)=0. The matrix c is therefore additive: comparing permutations mapping distinct rows i,k to columns j,l and to l,j (other assignments unchanged) gives c_ij+c_kl=c_il+c_kj; such permutations exist for all distinct row and column pairs. With zero diagonal this implies c_ij=a_i−a_j, fixing a_1=0. In particular the kernel maps injectively and linearly over F₃ into F₃^(n−1) via its first row (every paired relation holds mod 3). The resulting additivity over the integers is stronger than the dimension bound alone.

More generally, let p be an odd prime dividing a uniform q. Use the same tree matrix over the field Fₚ. Lift each left-kernel coefficient to [−(p−1)/2,(p−1)/2]. The dot product on any arc has absolute value at most p−1, hence vanishes over the integers, so the same zero-diagonal additive equations hold. The first-column restriction is injective. If the kernel had dimension at least two, two coordinate functionals indexed i and j in that column would be independent. Prescribe their lifts as +(p−1)/2 and −(p−1)/2. Then the integer identity cᵢⱼ=cᵢ₁−cⱼ₁ gives p−1, outside the lift interval. Thus the nullity is at most one and m≥C+(n²−n)−1≥n²−n. Taking p=q=3 proves the stated sharper lower bound; the preceding subdivision gives its upper bound.

## Counting bound with a common modulus

For general entry degree ℓ, suppose each target exponent qₑ is divisible by a common integer g>ℓ. Use the same switch potentials and tree column data modulo g. Inject coefficient arrays with c∈{0,...,B}, B=floor((g−1)/ℓ), vanishing on reference diagonal and in first row, into tree column data modulo g. On collision, difference lift in [−B,B] has integer dot absolute ≤Bℓ<g on every arc, zero mod g hence integer zero. Every target permutation therefore gives weighted additive q_e c_e, and vanishing first row/diagonal implies all zero. Thus (B+1)^((n−1)^2)≤g^(m−C), m≥1+ceil((n−1)^2 log_g(B+1)).

## Weighted modular forest bound

Expand each entry as parallel edges labeled by monomials of degree at most \(\ell\). Every labeled matching gives a target monomial by nonnegativity. Contract the pairs of a reference labeled matching contributing a chosen target monomial. An arc is labeled by the chosen alternative entry monomial. Delete unused labeled edges. Every remaining arc belongs to a directed switch cycle by symmetric difference, so the directed graph is a union of strongly connected components. Loops for unused-in-reference alternative parallel terms count as cycles of length one.

Fix an original variable \(x_e\) absent from the reference. Assign an arc the exponent \(a_e\) of that variable in its label. The sum on any directed simple cycle is either \(0\) or \(q\), because it gives a reference switch. The sum on a closed walk is thus divisible by \(q\), yielding a well-defined potential \(\phi_e\) into the residues modulo \(q\) in each component. A symmetric-difference cycle with a matching that uses this variable has total weight exactly \(q\). The sequence of its residues visits a set with every cyclic gap at most \(\ell\), since its increments lie in \([0,\ell]\) and it winds once.

Choose one undirected spanning tree in each component, remembering which arc witnessed each tree edge. In the component of this cycle, replace the projected endpoints of every \(e\)-weighted tree edge by a path around the clockwise residue cycle of length \(a_e\), from its arc's tail potential to its head potential. This means a constant edge when \(a_e=0\). The union is connected and contains all vertex potentials in the component. It therefore contains the residues visited by the witness cycle. A connected subgraph of the residue cycle containing all the witness residues has at least \(q-\ell\) edges. Indeed, it is either the entire cycle or a path whose missing run of edges fits in a single cyclic gap. View the residue cycle at \(q=2\) as a two-edge multigraph. Thus the sum of the \(e\)-exponents over all tree edges is at least \(q-\ell\). Summing over the \(n^2-n\) absent variables uses at most \(\ell\) total exponents per tree edge. This proves \((m-1)\ell\ge(n^2-n)(q-\ell)\). The same proof gives \((m-1)\ell\ge\sum_e(q_e-\ell)\) for mixed powers, summing absent variables whose \(q_e>\ell\).

## Exact order of a sum of disjoint pure powers

For \(F=\sum_{i=0}^{b-1}c_i x_i^{q_i}\), with \(c_i>0\), \(q_i>\ell\), let \(L_i=\lceil q_i/\ell\rceil\). For \(b\ge3\) the least order is \(1+\sum_i(L_i-1)\). For \(b=1,2\) it is \(\max_i L_i\). This is the bounded-degree version of the exact Fermat argument in [PR #103](https://github.com/derekblair/math-proofs/pull/103).

A labeled matching is monochromatic after omitting constants. Every used nonconstant edge is a pure power with degree at most \(\ell\), since it appears in a monochromatic matching. Its variable is its color. Take a reference matching of color zero. All its at least \(L_0\) nonconstant edges mark vertices of the contracted graph. Comparing it with a selected matching of any other color \(j\) gives exactly one simple switch cycle containing all marks and all nonconstant edges of that matching. In fact any component adding a color-\(j\) edge must remove all reference marks, and switching it gives the complete color-\(j\) degree. Its at least \(L_j\) nonconstant arcs are distributed along the cycle.

The union of the selected nonreference cycles has no off-reference color-zero arcs. A simple directed cycle intersecting a nonempty proper subset of marks would retain some, but not all, color-zero degree, so it is impossible. A cycle avoiding all marks has only constant arcs because it retains the full degree of reference color zero. The marked vertices therefore have the same cyclic order on the selected cycles. Otherwise, after linearizing at one mark, an inverted pair gives a closed walk through a mark avoiding the linearization mark, erasing only internal repetitions to give a forbidden cycle. The interiors of paths joining distinct successive mark pairs are also disjoint. An intersection would splice the initial leg of one path to the final leg of the other, then follow the reference cyclic order back to the starting mark, skipping a nonempty interval. Erase internal repetitions to get a forbidden cycle through that starting mark.

Select any one path for each successive mark pair to splice an allowable switch. All positive arcs of all chosen colors must occupy one common interval. Otherwise one could splice intervals from distinct nonreference colors and create a mixed-color matching. To see this even if one color uses two intervals, compare its positive intervals to any positive interval of another color, which exists because \(b\ge3\). Within the common interval, count the internal vertices immediately after the first \(L_j-1\) positive arcs of each color's selected path. A vertex counted for two different colors would splice a prefix of one to the suffix of the other with both colors present. Erasing internal repetitions removes only constant cycles since they avoid marks. The spliced path would complete with the other intervals to give a forbidden mixed-color matching. The counted vertices are private to each color. There are thus at least \(L_0+\sum_{j>0}(L_j-1)\) total vertices.

For equality use \(L_0\) reference marks and one cycle through the reference marks per nonreference color: all cycles share the same ordered constant path through the marks, and each uses its own return path with \(L_j\) positive arcs for each nonreference branch. Label the arcs by successive variable powers of degree at most \(\ell\) summing to \(q_j\), and put the coefficient \(c_j\) on one of them. The reference diagonal is a product of \(L_0\) bounded-degree factors carrying \(c_0 x_0^{q_0}\); auxiliary diagonal entries are one. For two colors use diagonal and full-cycle alternatives of order \(\max_i L_i\), padding the shorter alternative with constants; for one color use diagonal entries only. The degree bound shows optimality in those cases.

For the Hall slice, split the rows into groups of sizes \(u,n-u\), and the columns into groups of sizes \(u-1,n-u+1\). Put ones in the top-left and bottom-right blocks, zeros in the bottom-left block, and distinct variables in the top-right block. Every matching uses exactly one top-right entry, with \((u-1)!(n-u)!\) completions per entry. Hence the slice is a constant multiple of a Fermat sum on \(u(n-u+1)\) variables; maximizing over \(u\) gives \(\lfloor(n+1)^2/4\rfloor\). Substitution never increases the representing entry degree.


## Coefficientwise limits and verification

The same lower bounds apply to coefficientwise limits at fixed order and fixed entry degree. For each approximant with positive permanent at the all-ones input, remove each entry not used by a positive matching. The matrix of entry evaluations then has total support and can be scaled by positive row and column factors to doubly stochastic form ([Sinkhorn–Knopp](https://doi.org/10.2140/pjm.1967.21.343)). Scale one row again to restore the original value of the permanent at all ones. This factor is bounded along the sequence by the van der Waerden lower bound for doubly stochastic matrices of fixed order. The combined scalings preserve the entire original permanent polynomial because they restore the product of the row and column scale factors to one. Every normalized entry coefficient is nonnegative and bounded by its entry's value at all ones. A convergent subsequence in this fixed finite-dimensional space gives an exact representation of the limit.

[Sheshadri](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40484) considers bounded-degree determinantal and Pfaffian representations, a different model. [The affine-projection dossier](https://github.com/derekblair/math-proofs/pull/105) concerns degree one. Here the entries may contain mixed monomials, but negative coefficients, arbitrary signed projections, and permanent evaluation algorithms are outside scope.
